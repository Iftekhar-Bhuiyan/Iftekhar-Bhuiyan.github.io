Executive Summary

Academic failure is rarely random. Behind every failed unit or withdrawn student is a pattern — a combination of attendance habits, work commitments, family background, and support-seeking behaviour that, when viewed together, reliably separates students who succeed from those who don't.

This project analyses 2,500 university student records across three academic years to answer a question that matters to every institution: which factors most strongly predict academic success — and what can universities do about them?

⚡ Central Finding

Attendance rate has the strongest single correlation with GPA (r = 0.415). Students attending 90–100% of classes average a GPA of 2.59, while those below 50% average just 1.52 — a 70% performance gap driven by one observable, actionable behaviour.

The Challenge

The university's Academic Board raised concerns after three consecutive years of declining completion rates across several faculties. A routine audit revealed that over 30% of students were flagged as academically at-risk by mid-semester — yet the existing early-warning system relied on end-of-semester grades alone, making intervention chronically late.

The Analytics team was asked to produce two things: a ranked model of the factors that predict academic success or failure, and a live Power BI dashboard that academic advisors could use to identify at-risk students before it was too late to act.

Why This Matters

Each student withdrawal costs a university an estimated $8,000–$15,000 in lost tuition revenue. The 65 withdrawals in this dataset represent a potential $500K–$1M in annual preventable loss — before accounting for reputational, equity, and student welfare impacts.

Methodology

1
Data Collection & Variable Selection
Compiled 25 variables spanning student demographics, entry pathways, financial circumstances, behavioural factors (attendance, study hours), academic background (prior GPA), support service engagement, and final outcomes across AY2022–2024.
2
Data Cleaning & Feature Engineering (Python)
Used Pandas to handle missing values, standardise categorical encodings, and engineer derived fields: attendance_band, at_risk_flag, and composite_score (a weighted index combining attendance, study hours, and prior GPA). Validated distributions against published sector benchmarks.
3
Exploratory SQL Analysis (10 Targeted Queries)
Wrote 10 SQL queries isolating the impact of each factor on GPA and failure rate: attendance banding, employment load, first-generation status, socioeconomic income, entry pathway, scholarship type, and support services uptake.
4
Predictor Ranking (GPA Lift Analysis)
Quantified each factor's impact using a GPA lift metric: the difference in average GPA between the best and worst subgroup for each variable. This produces an intuitive, stakeholder-friendly ranking without requiring a formal regression model.
5
Power BI Dashboard — 4 Pages
Built a cross-filtered interactive report: (1) Institutional Overview, (2) Predictor Analysis deep-dive, (3) Faculty & Cohort Benchmarking, (4) At-Risk Student Register — a row-level watchlist for academic advisors, with conditional formatting based on risk tier.

💡 Key Insight

The top two predictors — attendance and employment load — are behavioural and institutional, not fixed. Unlike family income or parental education, they are directly addressable through scheduling, policy, and proactive outreach. This means universities have genuine leverage over the factors that matter most.

Key Findings

01

Attendance Below 60% Is Almost Always Predictive of Failure

Students attending fewer than 60% of classes average a GPA of just 1.70 — below the 2.0 passing threshold at most institutions. The GPA improves steadily with every attendance band, peaking at 2.59 for 90–100% attendees. Crucially, this relationship holds across all faculties and year levels, making attendance the most universally reliable early warning signal available.

02

Full-Time Work + Full-Time Study Is an Academic Crisis Point

Students juggling full-time employment (20+ hrs/week) while studying full-time fail at a rate of 47.6% — nearly double the cohort average of 23.2%. This group (311 students) also recorded the highest withdrawal rate at 10.8%. Part-time students who work full-time show a similar elevated risk, suggesting workload management — not just enrolment mode — is the driving factor.

03

First-Generation Students Fail at 57% Higher Rate Than Their Peers

Students whose parents hold no tertiary qualification (first-generation) fail at 32.2%, compared to 20.6% for non-first-gen peers — a gap of 11.6 percentage points that has persisted across all three academic years. Despite being among the highest-need group, only a small proportion access equity scholarships or support services, indicating an awareness or accessibility barrier.

First-gen fail rate: 32.2% Non-first-gen: 20.6% Gap: +11.6 percentage points
04

Mature Age Entry Students Have the Highest Fail Rate of All Pathways

Students entering via Mature Age Entry fail at 29.7% and withdraw at 3.2% — the worst outcomes of any entry pathway. This cohort likely faces the compounding pressures of family responsibilities, employment, and longer time out of academic environments. They also achieve the lowest distinction rate (4.1%), suggesting the challenge is systemic, not individual.

Mature Age fail rate: 29.7% ATAR (HS) fail rate: 21.0%
05

Students Using Multiple Support Services Outperform Those Using None

Students engaged with multiple support services (tutoring + advisor + counselling) average a GPA of 2.42 versus 2.11 for those using no services — a 0.31-point lift. Importantly, the type of support matters: Academic Advisor engagement produces the highest GPA lift (+0.38), suggesting that structured academic guidance outperforms pastoral support alone in driving outcomes.

Power BI Build Guide — Page Structure

Page 1 — Overview: 4 KPI cards + employment fail rate bar + attendance GPA bar + outcome donut.
Page 2 — Predictors: GPA lift ranked bar chart + scatter (attendance vs GPA) + income band matrix.
Page 3 — Faculty Benchmarking: Clustered bar (fail rate by faculty) + YoY trend line + cohort slicer.
Page 4 — Risk Register: Conditional-format table of at-risk students, filterable by faculty and risk tier. All pages cross-filter on: Faculty | Year Level | Academic Year | Study Mode.

Recommendations

Five evidence-based interventions — ranked by expected student impact and implementation feasibility.

HIGH PRIORITY
Automated Attendance Alert at 70% Threshold
Trigger an automatic email to the student and their academic advisor when attendance drops below 70%. The data shows GPA degrades sharply below this level — making it the optimal intervention point before outcomes deteriorate.
Est. impact: reduce fail rate by 4–6 pp
HIGH PRIORITY
Flexible Scheduling for Full-Time Workers
Introduce evening and asynchronous delivery options for high-enrolment units in faculties with the most full-time working students. The 47.6% failure rate for this group is a scheduling problem as much as a capacity one.
Target group: 311 at-risk students
HIGH PRIORITY
First-Generation Student Outreach Programme
Proactively assign a peer mentor and academic advisor to all incoming first-generation students at enrolment — not on request. The data shows they seek support at lower rates despite needing it most.
32.2% fail rate → target <22%
MEDIUM PRIORITY
Mandatory Academic Advisor Check-In for Mature Age Students
Require all Mature Age Entry students to complete an advisor session in Week 3 of their first semester. This pathway has the highest fail and withdrawal rate, and the lowest distinction rate — early engagement has the greatest potential return.
Targets 29.7% fail rate cohort
ONGOING
Embed the Risk Register in Weekly Advisor Workflow
Publish the Power BI dashboard to all academic advisors on Power BI Service. Set weekly automated email snapshots of the Risk Register sorted by CRITICAL → HIGH → ELEVATED so outreach is always prioritised to the students who need it most.
Converts analysis into daily action

Lessons Learned

The most challenging part of this project wasn't the SQL — it was resisting the urge to over-complicate. The strongest predictor (attendance) is also the simplest to measure and the easiest for anyone to understand. A regression model might have achieved a marginally better R² — but a GPA lift table gets acted on. Always match analytical complexity to the decision it needs to support.

  • Correlation ≠ causation, but it's enough to act. Attendance correlates with GPA, but low attendance may also be a symptom of external stress. The recommendation accounts for this: the alert triggers a conversation, not a penalty.
  • First-generation data is systematically under-collected. Parental education was the variable with the most missing data in the raw set — which is itself an equity signal worth flagging.
  • Dashboard audiences need one question per page. Early versions of the Power BI report tried to show everything on Page 1. Breaking it into four focused pages dramatically improved the advisors' ability to act quickly on the risk register.
